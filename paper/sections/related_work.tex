\section{Background}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Iterative paraphrasing and authorship.}
In the work done by Tripto et al.~\cite{tripto2024ship} at ACL 2024, the authors ask whether
a text keeps its original authorship after repeated paraphrasing. They apply four paraphrasers
(ChatGPT, PaLM2, Pegasus, DIPPER) iteratively over seven datasets, re-running attribution and
MGT detectors at each generation. They found that attribution accuracy degrades monotonically
with each round; however, only aggregate accuracy figures are reported, and the specific features
driving decay are never isolated---a gap our work directly addresses.

\paragraph{Paraphrase as an evasion attack.}
In the work done by Krishna et al.~\cite{krishna2023paraphrasing} at NeurIPS 2023, the concern
is detector robustness to evasion. They train DIPPER, an 11B-parameter discourse-level paraphraser
with controllable lexical diversity and reordering, and propose a retrieval-based defense. A single
DIPPER pass drives DetectGPT and GPTZero toward chance; however, the retrieval defense presumes
the generating API logs its outputs, an assumption that is often unrealistic in practice.

\paragraph{Zero-shot MGT detection.}
In the work done by Mitchell et al.~\cite{mitchell2023detectgpt} at ICML 2023, the goal is
detecting machine-generated text without labeled data. DetectGPT compares a candidate text's
log-likelihood against those of small perturbations, exploiting negative curvature in the
probability surface. It outperforms prior zero-shot baselines, but requires white-box likelihoods
and collapses entirely under even a single paraphrase pass.

\paragraph{Attribution across neural generators.}
In the work done by Uchendu et al.~\cite{uchendu2020authorship} at EMNLP 2020, authorship
attribution is extended beyond human writers to neural text generators and cast as three subtasks.
They benchmark stylometric, deep-learning, and transformer classifiers across nineteen generators.
Generators remain separable at generation zero; however, only single-pass text is tested, leaving
open whether generator signatures survive iterative rewriting.

\paragraph{Content-independent author representations.}
In the work done by Rivera-Soto et al.~\cite{riverasoto2021learning} at EMNLP 2021, the authors
ask whether a single authorship representation can transfer across topic domains. They train a
Transformer encoder contrastively on Reddit, Amazon, and fan-fiction so that same-author documents
embed close together. The embeddings transfer well across topics; however, they remain sensitive
to topic overlap, a style--content confound that our fixed-source-document design removes.

\paragraph{Measuring semantic retention.}
In the work done by Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2020bertscore} at ICLR 2020, the problem is
evaluating text generation beyond $n$-gram overlap. BERTScore greedily matches candidate and
reference tokens using contextual embeddings, tracking human adequacy judgments far better than
BLEU. BERTScore is blind to style; we therefore pair it with explicit stylometry to separate
the two axes of identity decay.

\paragraph{Distributional gap between neural and human text.}
In the work done by Pillutla et al.~\cite{pillutla2021mauve} at NeurIPS 2021, MAUVE is
introduced as a metric that quantifies the distributional gap between neural-generated and
human text via information-theoretic divergences. MAUVE correlates well with human quality
judgments and captures fluency degradation invisible to $n$-gram metrics. We draw on MAUVE
as a complementary evaluation lens for later experiments at the corpus level.

\paragraph{Watermarking language model outputs.}
In the work done by Kirchenbauer et al.~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark} at ICML 2023, a
statistical watermark is embedded in LLM outputs by biasing token selection toward a
pseudo-random ``green'' token list at inference time. The watermark survives mild editing but
degrades under paraphrasing---directly connecting to our RQ3 on whether paraphraser fingerprints
are recoverable or erased by downstream editing.

\paragraph{Human difficulty in detecting generated text.}
In the work done by Ippolito et al.~\cite{ippolito2020automatic} at ACL 2020, the authors
study how generation quality (perplexity tier) and sampling strategy affect both human and
automatic detectability of machine-generated text. Humans are most easily fooled by fluent,
high-quality outputs; automatic classifiers remain more reliable. This calibrates our baseline
expectation: as paraphrasing improves fluency of already-fluent LLM text, detection should become
progressively harder.

\paragraph{Visual statistical detection.}
In the work done by Gehrmann et al.~\cite{gehrmann2019gltr} at ACL-SRW 2019, GLTR is presented
as a tool that visualises token-level statistical signatures of generation, highlighting tokens
that are unusually predictable given their context. Users assisted by GLTR detect generated text
at far higher rates than unaided humans. As paraphrase generations accumulate, such token-level
signatures are progressively destroyed, making GLTR-style detection increasingly unreliable---a
trend our decay curves can quantify.
